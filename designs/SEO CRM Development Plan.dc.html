SEO CRM — Stage-Wise Development Plan

A single system for client management, SEO project delivery, team capacity, backlink production, and performance data from Google Search Console, GA4, and Meta.

Draft v1 · September 2026 · Planning document, not a build commitment

1. Where we are starting from

The existing tracker is already a working multi-user application, not a prototype. It runs on Supabase with Google sign-in, an allowlist of team members, projects, assignments, a monthly sitewide checklist per project, an off-page module with a 200-link monthly target across 16 activity types, keyword lists with rank checks, and realtime sync so two people see the same board. That is a genuine Stage 0 asset: the data model for delivery already exists and the team already uses it.

What it is not yet is a CRM. It has no client entity (only projects), no commercial layer (retainer, renewal, invoice), no capacity or skills model, no live performance data from Search Console or GA4, no backlink lifecycle beyond a count, no reporting output, and no client-facing view. It also has three engineering issues to fix before anything is built on top: authorisation is enforced only in the browser, every data load rebuilds the whole page (which can wipe a teammate's in-progress note), and read failures are silent.

The plan below keeps the existing database and grows it. Nothing gets thrown away.

2. What the reference products tell us

The eleven products you shared fall into four groups, and each group contributes a different part of the target system.

Sales CRMs (Pipedrive, SugarCRM, Nutshell). These own the pre-sale half: pipelines, deal stages, activity history, forecasting. Pipedrive's own SEO pitch is that the right platform adapts to the agency's workflow, integrates with SEO and reporting tools, and centralises client, lead and communication data, with workflow automation and project templates replacing admin work. Take from them: the deal pipeline, the contact/activity timeline, and automated follow-up reminders.

Client-portal all-in-ones (SuiteDash, HoneyBook, Bonsai, GrowCRM). These own the post-sale relationship: proposals, contracts, invoicing, file sharing, white-label portals. SuiteDash's argument is that a contact created in the CRM is immediately available to invoicing, estimates, proposals, scheduling and every other toolkit — the same data rather than a copy, with no connector to authorise and no sync step to debug. Take from them: one shared client record across delivery and billing, and a branded client login.

Work management (ClickUp, seocrm.com, afternorth). These own the delivery engine: templates, recurring tasks, time tracking, workload. ClickUp frames the agency-specific needs as assigning tasks based on skill set, availability or workload; scheduling automated performance reports; and alerting on task deadlines and milestones. seocrm.com's project module similarly creates auto and recurring tasks, assigns and tracks progress, and bills by project, hour, month or performance. Take from them: the SEO project template library, recurring monthly workstreams, per-task hour logging, and the workload view.

SEO data (Mangools). Keyword research, rank tracking, SERP and backlink analysis. We do not rebuild this — we ingest from it and from the free Google APIs, then present it inside the client record.

The gap worth building. None of these products ties SEO performance movement to the work that caused it and the person who did it. A CRM that answers "this client is down 18% in clicks, here is which deliverables slipped, who owns them, and what we are doing this month" is the thing that does not exist off the shelf. That is the spine of this plan.

4. Stage detail

Stage 0 — Harden the foundation · 2 weeks

Nothing new for users; everything that makes the next eight stages safe.

  • Row-level security on every table, with policies mirroring the roles (admin, manager, member, client). Today the browser decides who is an admin; the database must decide.
  • Replace full-page re-render with targeted updates, so realtime edits no longer interrupt someone typing a note.
  • Error and empty states on every read; no more silent failures that look like no data.
  • Audit log table: who changed what, when. Required later for client-facing accountability.
  • Environment split (staging vs production) and database migrations under version control.

Exit criteria: a non-allowlisted Google account can sign in and retrieve nothing; two people can edit the same project simultaneously without data loss.

Stage 1 — CRM core · 3–4 weeks

Introduce the client as the top-level record. Projects become children of clients rather than standalone rows.

  • Accounts: company, website, industry, timezone, health flag, account manager, start date, contract end, retainer value, scope summary.
  • Contacts: multiple people per account with role, email, phone, preferred channel.
  • Lead pipeline: stages from enquiry to proposal to won/lost, with value, source, expected close date, and loss reason.
  • Activity timeline: calls, meetings, emails logged, notes, file attachments — one chronological record per account.
  • Renewal watch: contract end dates surfaced 90/60/30 days out.

Exit criteria: every existing project is attached to an account; the account page shows commercial terms, contacts, and live delivery status side by side.

Stage 2 — Project & task engine · 4 weeks

Generalise today's fixed monthly checklist into a template system, and let team members record their own hours.

  • Template library: technical audit, on-page optimisation, content production, off-page campaign, migration, local SEO, monthly retainer. Each template carries tasks, default owners by role, estimated hours, and priority.
  • Recurring workstreams: the current monthly sitewide checklist becomes one recurring template instance, generated automatically on the 1st.
  • Tasks: assignee, watcher, due date, estimate, dependency, status, priority, checklist, comments, attachments.
  • Hour logging: each member logs time against a task — start/stop timer or manual entry, with a weekly timesheet view and manager approval. This feeds capacity in Stage 3 and profitability in Stage 7.
  • Views: list, kanban by stage, calendar by due date, and a per-person "my week".

Exit criteria: a new client can be onboarded from a template in under five minutes; a week of logged hours reconciles against the timesheet total.

Stage 3 — Team management, strength & capacity · 3 weeks

The answer to "who should take this, and can they?"

  • Skills matrix: each member rated across technical SEO, content, outreach, analytics, local, client communication. This is the "team work strength" view — it also exposes single points of failure where only one person can do a thing.
  • Availability: weekly contracted hours, leave, holidays, part-time patterns.
  • Allocation planner: drag a project onto a person; the system warns when the assignment pushes them past capacity, and suggests who has room and the right skill.
  • Utilisation dashboard: booked vs capacity per person and per team, billable vs internal split, trend over weeks.
  • Performance: tasks completed on time, checklist completion rate, average turnaround, links delivered vs target.

Exit criteria: the planner refuses to silently overbook anyone, and a manager can staff a new retainer in one screen.

Stage 4 — Search performance: GSC and GA4 · 4–5 weeks

Where "project up or down" gets answered with data instead of opinion. Each account connects its own Google properties via OAuth; a nightly job pulls and stores the numbers so history survives Google's own retention limits.

  • Search Console: clicks, impressions, CTR, average position — by date, query, page, country and device; index coverage and validation status; Core Web Vitals; manual actions and security issues.
  • GA4: sessions and users by channel, organic landing-page performance, engagement rate, conversions and conversion value, lead events, assisted conversions.
  • Rank tracking: keep the existing manual keyword checks, and allow import from Mangools or a similar source so the two sit in one table.
  • Health scorecard: per account, a month-over-month and year-over-year delta on the five metrics that matter, rolled into a single up/flat/down signal on the client card.
  • Alerts: clicks down more than a set threshold week-over-week, indexation drop, position loss on a priority keyword, conversion tracking stopped firing.
  • Annotations: mark deployments, migrations, algorithm updates and content launches on the timeline, so a movement can be explained by the work next to it.

Exit criteria: opening any account shows last month's organic performance without anyone logging into Google; an alert fires within 24 hours of a real drop.

Stage 5 — Backlink production · 3 weeks

Today's off-page module counts activities against a 200-per-month target. This turns each count into a tracked object with a lifecycle.

  • Link record: prospect domain, contact, authority metric, relevance, target URL, anchor text, link type (guest post, directory, profile, web 2.0, PR, niche edit), cost, owner.
  • Pipeline: prospected → contacted → negotiating → content sent → published → verified live → submitted for indexing → indexed. Each transition timestamped and attributed.
  • Quality control: duplicate-domain check across all clients, anchor-text distribution warning, flags for links that disappear or go nofollow on re-check.
  • Targets: monthly quota per client and per activity type, carried over from the current model, with progress and forecast.
  • Outreach log: emails sent, replies, response rate per prospect list and per team member.

Exit criteria: every link the team claims can be clicked, verified live, and traced to who built it and what it cost.

Stage 6 — Social and lead capture · 3 weeks

  • Meta connection: Facebook Page and Instagram professional account per client, pulling reach, engagement, follower growth, and post-level performance.
  • Paid: campaign spend, impressions, clicks, leads and cost per lead, reconciled against GA4 conversions.
  • Content calendar: planned posts with owner, status and approval, so social sits in the same workload as SEO tasks.
  • UTM builder: enforced naming so channel attribution in GA4 stays clean.
  • Lead routing: form and Meta lead-ad submissions land in the Stage 1 pipeline with source intact.

Exit criteria: a client's monthly lead count can be broken down by channel without opening any external tool.

Stage 7 — Reporting, client portal and billing · 4 weeks

The payoff stage: the data assembled in 4–6 becomes the thing the client sees, and the thing that gets invoiced.

  • Report builder: white-label monthly report combining performance deltas, work completed, links built, keywords moved, and next month's plan. Exportable to PDF and schedulable by email.
  • Client portal: read-only login showing progress, approved deliverables, reports and invoices, with a comment thread. Nothing internal — no hours, no margins.
  • Billing: retainer schedule, one-off projects, expenses (link costs, tools), invoice generation, payment status.
  • Profitability: retainer value minus logged hours at cost rate minus expenses, per account per month.

Exit criteria: the monthly report for every client is produced without manual spreadsheet work.

Stage 8 — Intelligence · ongoing

Forecasting traffic against target, anomaly detection that separates a real drop from seasonality, AI-written first drafts of report commentary, churn-risk scoring from health plus engagement, and a public API with webhooks for anything not built in.

6. Roles and permissions

Admin sees everything including rates, margins and invoices. Manager sees their accounts, full delivery and capacity data, but not company-wide financials. Member sees their assigned projects and tasks, logs their own hours, and cannot view rates or other people's timesheets. Client sees only the portal: their own progress, reports and invoices. Every rule is enforced in the database, not the interface.

7. Decisions needed before Stage 1

  1. Build or buy. Stages 1, 2 and 7 are largely commodity. If the goal is speed, an off-the-shelf CRM plus a custom SEO-data layer gets you there faster. If the goal is a product you own and can sell later, building is justified. This changes everything downstream.
  2. Internal tool or product. Multi-tenancy, billing and onboarding only matter if you intend to sell it.
  3. Rank data source. Mangools, DataForSEO, or continue manual checks. Affects Stage 4 cost and scope.
  4. Backlink metrics source. Authority scores require a paid API (Ahrefs, Moz, Majestic). Decide before Stage 5.
  5. Team size and accounts to support. Sets the capacity model and the API quota budget.
  6. Client portal in scope or not. It roughly doubles Stage 7 and adds a permanent support burden.

8. Recommended first move

Do Stage 0 regardless of the build-or-buy decision — the current tool is in daily use and the authorisation gap is real today.

In parallel, I can design the Stage 1–3 screens (account record, project workspace, capacity planner) so you can see the system before committing engineering time to it. Designs first, then the build order is easy to defend.
